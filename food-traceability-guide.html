Understanding restaurant food traceability

Batches, allergens and the record an inspector asks for: what traceability means in a restaurant kitchen, and how to keep it without a paper diary.

What traceability means in a kitchen

Traceability is being able to answer one question quickly: where did this come from, and where did it go? For a restaurant that means knowing which supplier and which batch every ingredient came from, when it arrived, and which dishes it went into.

The principle food law uses is often called one step back, one step forward. You need to know who supplied you (one step back), and businesses that supply other businesses need to know who they supplied (one step forward). A restaurant selling straight to guests mainly needs the first half, but knowing which dishes a batch went into is what makes a recall manageable.

What the law asks of a restaurant

In the UK, food businesses must be able to identify the businesses that supplied them with food and ingredients, and show those records to the authorities when asked. This comes from general food law that was carried into UK law after Brexit, alongside the Food Safety Act 1990.

In practice, an inspector will expect a food safety management system based on HACCP principles. Small businesses in England and Wales often use the Food Standards Agency’s Safer Food, Better Business pack; Scotland has CookSafe; Northern Ireland has Safe Catering.

This guide is general information, not legal advice. Check the Food Standards Agency (or Food Standards Scotland) and your local authority for what applies to your business.

Allergens: the 14 and the three honest states

UK food law names 14 allergens that must be declared when they are used as ingredients:

  • Celery
  • Cereals containing gluten
  • Crustaceans
  • Eggs
  • Fish
  • Lupin
  • Milk
  • Molluscs
  • Mustard
  • Tree nuts
  • Peanuts
  • Sesame
  • Soya
  • Sulphur dioxide and sulphites

For food that is not prepacked, which covers most of what a restaurant serves, you must be able to tell guests which of these each dish contains. Food that is prepacked for direct sale, such as sandwiches made and packed on site, has needed a full ingredient label with allergens emphasised since October 2021, under what is known as Natasha’s Law.

Contains, free from, not checked

The hardest part of allergen information is not the list; it is keeping it true when a supplier swaps a product. A useful habit is to record every allergen for every dish in one of three states: contains, confirmed free from (with the date someone checked), or not checked. “Not checked” is honest, and it tells the team exactly what to verify before telling a guest a dish is safe.

What an inspector asks for

Food hygiene ratings in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, and the Food Hygiene Information Scheme in Scotland, are based on inspections by local authority officers, often called EHOs. They look at how food is handled, the condition of the premises, and how confident they can be that safety is managed. Records are a large part of that confidence.

RecordWhat it shows
Supplier list and delivery checksWhere food came from, and that it arrived in a safe condition
Fridge and freezer temperaturesCold food kept at 8°C or below (most kitchens aim for 5°C or below)
Cooking and hot holdingFood cooked through, and held hot at 63°C or above
Cleaning schedulesWho cleaned what, and when
Allergen informationWhat each dish contains, kept up to date
TrainingStaff know the procedures they are following

The common thread is that a record written at the time is worth far more than one written up afterwards. A check recorded with a name and a time as it happens is evidence; a page filled in on Sunday is a memory.

Batches, use-by and best before

A use-by date is about safety: food should not be eaten after it, even if it looks and smells fine. A best before date is about quality: food is safe after it, but may not be at its best. In a kitchen, use-by is the date that matters for chilled ingredients like dairy, meat and fish.

Recording the batch or lot code at delivery is what links an ingredient to its use-by, its supplier and the dishes it went into. It takes seconds at the door and saves hours if anything goes wrong later.

Run a recall drill

Suppliers sometimes recall a batch, and the Food Standards Agency publishes allergy alerts and product recalls. Try this once with your team: pick a real ingredient, invent a recall for the batch you received last week, and time how long it takes to answer three questions.

  1. Did we receive that batch, and when?
  2. Which dishes and which services used it?
  3. How much is still on the shelf, and where?

If the answers mean searching delivery notes, the bin and people’s memories, you have found the gap. If they come from one record, you are in good shape.

What to try this week

  • Scan or write down the batch code of every delivery for one week.
  • Record fridge temperatures with a name and time, as they are taken.
  • Mark every dish’s allergens as contains, confirmed free from (dated) or not checked.
  • Run the recall drill above with one ingredient.
